# Philosophy of mind

## Aristotle got there first, and differently

*De Anima* treats the soul (*psuchē*) not as a thing inhabiting a body but as
the **form** of a living body — its organisation, its capacity to do what
living things do. On this **hylomorphic** view the question "how do soul and
body interact?" is confused, like asking how the shape of a statue interacts
with its bronze.

The soul has nested capacities: nutritive (plants), perceptive and appetitive
(animals), rational (humans). Perception is the reception of *form without
matter* — the eye takes on the form of what is seen without becoming it.

This matters because it sidesteps the problem Descartes created. Aristotle has
no mind–body problem, because he has no mind–body dualism. Contemporary
**embodied** and **enactive** approaches to cognition are, quite consciously,
a return to something like this position.

## The problem Descartes made

**Substance dualism**: mind and body are distinct substances, thinking and
extended. Its fatal difficulty was raised immediately by **Princess Elisabeth
of Bohemia** in correspondence (1643) — how can an unextended substance cause
motion in an extended one? Descartes never answered adequately. The objection
is still the decisive one, and Elisabeth deserves the credit she is usually
denied.

Positions since:

- **Behaviourism** — mental states are dispositions to behave. Collapsed on
  the circularity problem (a belief only issues in behaviour given desires,
  and vice versa) and on its denial of the inner.
- **Type identity** — mental types *are* brain types. Undermined by
  **multiple realisability** (Putnam): pain in a human, an octopus and a
  hypothetical silicon being would be different physical types.
- **Functionalism** — mental states are defined by their causal roles
  (inputs, other states, outputs). The dominant view; substrate-independent,
  which is why it underwrites the possibility of machine minds.
- **Eliminative materialism** (the Churchlands) — folk-psychological states
  like belief and desire may simply not exist, as phlogiston did not.
- **Property dualism** — one substance, irreducibly mental properties.
- **Panpsychism** — experience is fundamental and ubiquitous. Taken more
  seriously in the last two decades than for a century, mainly because the
  alternatives struggle; its own difficulty is the **combination problem**.

## The hard problem

**Chalmers (1995)** separated the *easy* problems (discrimination,
integration, reportability, attention — hard in practice, but clearly
functional) from the **hard problem**: why is any of this accompanied by
subjective experience at all? Why is there something it is like?

The supporting arguments:

- **Nagel**, "What is it like to be a bat?" (1974) — we can know everything
  about echolocation and still not know what it is like.
- **Jackson's Mary** — a colour scientist who knows every physical fact about
  colour vision but has only seen black and white. On leaving the room, does
  she learn something new? If yes, there are non-physical facts. (Jackson
  later abandoned his own argument.)
- **Conceivability** — a zombie physically identical to me but lacking
  experience seems conceivable; if conceivable then possible, physicalism is
  false. The disputed step is conceivability-to-possibility.

Against: **Dennett** denies the hard problem is a problem, arguing that once
all the functional facts are explained, nothing remains but an illusion of
something remaining — the **illusionist** position, developed by Frankish.
**Higher-order theories** (Rosenthal) make a state conscious by being
represented by another state. **Global workspace** (Baars, Dehaene) and
**integrated information theory** (Tononi) are the leading empirical
programmes, and both are better understood as addressing the easy problems
thoroughly than as dissolving the hard one.

The honest position: this is genuinely unresolved, and the disagreement is
not merely verbal. Anyone who tells you it is settled in either direction is
overselling.

## Intentionality

The "aboutness" of mental states — Brentano's mark of the mental. How can a
brain state be *about* something? Candidate answers: causal-informational
theories (Dretske, Fodor), teleosemantics (Millikan — content from
evolutionary function), interpretationism (Dennett's **intentional stance**:
attributing beliefs is a predictive strategy, not a discovery). The
**disjunction problem** — why does a cow-on-a-dark-night-triggered "horse"
representation mean *horse* and not *horse-or-cow* — remains the sharpest
obstacle.

## Extended and embodied mind

**Clark & Chalmers (1998)**: if a process would count as cognitive were it in
the head, its location does not change that. Otto's notebook does for him what
Inga's biological memory does for her; the notebook is part of his cognitive
system. The **parity principle** is the argument; the standard objection
(Adams & Aizawa) is that non-derived content is the mark of the cognitive and
notebooks lack it.

**Embodied cognition** more broadly holds that cognition is shaped by the
body's morphology and action capacities. Note the necessary caution: a large
part of the empirical literature invoked for strong embodiment claims —
metaphor-priming, bodily-state effects on judgement — is among the
worst-affected by the replication problems in [research-methods]. The
philosophical position is not thereby refuted, but its favourite evidence is
weaker than commonly presented. Gesture research (see
[nonverbal-behaviour]) is the sturdier support.

## Personal identity and the self

- **Psychological continuity** (Locke) — memory and connectedness make the
  person; vulnerable to Reid's brave-officer objection.
- **Biological / animalism** (Olson) — we are organisms; identity is
  organic continuity.
- **Narrative identity** (Ricoeur, MacIntyre, Schechtman) — a self is
  constituted by the story one tells. Connects directly to
  [aristotle-ethics], where a life is assessed as a whole.
- **Parfit** (*Reasons and Persons*) — identity is not what matters;
  what matters is psychological continuity, which admits of degrees and can
  branch. If so, much of our concern for our own future is misdirected.

Empirically: the sense of a unified self is substantially constructed. **Split-
brain** work (Sperry, Gazzaniga) and Gazzaniga's **interpreter** — the left
hemisphere confabulating explanations for behaviour it did not cause — bear
directly on this, as does **confabulation** research generally (Nisbett &
Wilson: people confidently report reasons for choices that demonstrably were
not the causes). That last finding is important for [manipulation-and-defence]:
if people routinely do not know why they chose, then "I wasn't influenced,
I decided" is not strong evidence of anything.

See [emotion-and-expression] for the affective side, [epistemology] for
knowledge of other minds, and [aristotle-ethics] for what a soul is *for*.
