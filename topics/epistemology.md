# Epistemology

The theory of knowledge matters here for a reason that is not obvious: the
techniques catalogued in [manipulation-and-defence] are attacks on someone's
epistemic position. Gaslighting is not merely cruel, it is an assault on a
person's standing as a knower. You cannot describe that precisely without the
vocabulary below.

## The classical analysis and its collapse

Knowledge was traditionally analysed as **justified true belief**: S knows
that p if and only if p is true, S believes p, and S is justified in
believing p.

**Gettier (1963)**, in three pages, produced counterexamples. Smith has strong
evidence that Jones will get the job and that Jones has ten coins in his
pocket, so he believes "the man who will get the job has ten coins in his
pocket." In fact Smith gets the job, and coincidentally has ten coins. The
belief is true, and justified, and is plainly not knowledge — it is true by
accident, and the justification does not connect to what makes it true.

Sixty years of repair attempts followed:

- **No false lemmas** — the justification must not pass through a false belief.
  Handles the original cases, fails on later ones (fake barn country).
- **Causal theory** (Goldman) — the fact must cause the belief. Struggles with
  mathematics and the future.
- **Reliabilism** — a belief is justified if produced by a reliable process.
  The dominant externalist position; faces the generality problem (how
  finely do you describe the process?) and the new evil demon problem.
- **Safety and sensitivity** — the belief must not easily have been false;
  or, had p been false, S would not have believed it. Sophisticated, and still
  contested.
- **Virtue epistemology** (Sosa, Zagzebski) — knowledge is true belief
  *because of* the exercise of intellectual virtue. Explicitly Aristotelian:
  it imports the structure of [aristotle-ethics] into knowing. Sosa's
  distinction between *apt* belief (true because competent) and merely
  accurate belief maps onto the Gettier intuition cleanly.
- **Knowledge first** (Williamson, 2000) — the project is backwards.
  Knowledge is a primitive mental state, not analysable into belief plus
  extras; belief is understood in terms of knowledge rather than the reverse.

The productive lesson is not any one repair. It is that *truth plus good
reasons is not enough* — the connection between the reasons and the truth has
to be non-accidental. That is exactly what manipulation severs: a manipulated
person can hold a true belief for reasons that would equally have produced a
false one.

## Internalism and externalism

**Internalism**: what justifies a belief must be accessible to the believer by
reflection. **Externalism**: justification can depend on facts outside the
believer's awareness — the reliability of their perceptual system, for
instance.

This bears directly on manipulation. On an internalist view, a manipulated
person's belief may be perfectly justified from the inside — they have done
everything right with the evidence they were given. On an externalist view it
is not justified, because the process generating it was unreliable *by
design*. Both capture something: the victim is blameless, and the belief is
still defective. The split explains why "you should have known better" is both
tempting and usually wrong.

## Testimony

Most of what anyone knows comes from others. Two positions:

- **Reductionism** (Hume) — testimony is justified only by one's own evidence
  that the source is reliable.
- **Anti-reductionism** (Reid, Coady) — we have a default entitlement to
  trust testimony absent defeaters, and reductionism cannot be satisfied
  without circularity, since checking sources itself relies on testimony.

The anti-reductionist is closer to right about how knowledge actually works,
which is why controlling someone's information environment is so effective:
isolate a person from independent testimony and you control what they can know
without ever lying to them directly. This is the mechanism behind the
"reality-testing isolation" entry in [manipulation-and-defence].

## Epistemic injustice

**Miranda Fricker (2007)** identified two wrongs done to people *as knowers*:

- **Testimonial injustice** — a speaker's word is given less credibility than
  it deserves because of prejudice about them. The hearer's credibility
  judgement is distorted by identity.
- **Hermeneutical injustice** — a gap in shared interpretive resources means
  someone cannot make sense of their own experience. Fricker's example:
  before "sexual harassment" existed as a concept, women experiencing it had
  no way to name it, to themselves or to others.

This is the sharpest available frame for what naming does. The observation in
[manipulation-and-defence] — that the most-reported mechanism in leaving a
coercive relationship is encountering the *name* for what was happening — is
the repair of a hermeneutical injustice. Concepts are epistemic infrastructure.

**Kate Abramson's** analysis of gaslighting fits here: its aim is not to win a
particular dispute but to destroy the target's standing as an independent
source of testimony, including to themselves. It is testimonial injustice
inflicted deliberately and privately.

## Scepticism, and why it is not the interesting part

The Cartesian demon, the brain in a vat, the dreaming argument. Responses:
Moore's "here is one hand"; contextualism (DeRose, Lewis — "knows" has
context-shifting standards); relevant alternatives (Dretske); Williamson's
externalism; the pragmatist dismissal.

For this corpus, radical scepticism is largely a distraction. The live problem
is not whether knowledge is possible in principle but how it is degraded in
practice — by unreliable testimony, motivated reasoning, engineered
information environments and prejudiced credibility judgements. That is
applied epistemology, and it is where the field has been most useful.

## What connects to the psychology

- **Motivated reasoning** — evidence is evaluated differently depending on
  whether it supports what one wants. Kunda's classic review; the effect is
  robust though its scope is debated.
- **Identity-protective cognition** (Kahan) — on politicised questions,
  *greater* numeracy and science literacy can increase polarisation, because
  ability is used to defend group identity. Uncomfortable and well-evidenced.
- **The illusion of explanatory depth** (Rozenblit & Keil) — people
  substantially overestimate how well they understand mechanisms. Asking them
  to explain how something works reduces confidence and moderates positions,
  which is one of the few debiasing interventions with support.
- **Bullshit receptivity** (Pennycook et al.) — measurable individual
  differences in willingness to find profundity in randomly generated
  pseudo-profound sentences, correlated with analytic thinking measures.
