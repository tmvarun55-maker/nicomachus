# The Rhetoric

Aristotle defines rhetoric as "the faculty of observing in any given case the
available means of persuasion" (I.2, 1355b). Three things follow from that
definition and are usually missed.

It is a **faculty of observing**, not of persuading — a capacity for analysis
first. This is what makes the Rhetoric a study text rather than a manual: its
subject is what *can* be persuasive, which is exactly what a critic needs to
know in order to see what is being done to them.

It is **counterpart to dialectic** (the opening line of the work). Dialectic
reasons from generally accepted opinions in dialogue; rhetoric does the same
thing in continuous speech to a public audience. Both are neutral arts.

And it is defined by the *available* means in a *given case* — rhetoric is
situational. There is no universally winning argument, which is why the work
spends so much time on audiences.

Aristotle wrote it partly against Plato, who in the *Gorgias* had called
rhetoric a knack for flattery, no more an art than cookery. Aristotle's reply
is that the art is neutral and its misuse is the misuse of a good thing:
"if it is argued that one who uses such power of speech unjustly might do
great harm, that is a charge which may be made in common against all good
things except virtue."

## The three pisteis (means of persuasion)

- **Ethos** — persuasion through the character the speaker projects *in the
  speech itself*. Aristotle is explicit (I.2, 1356a) that this must come from
  the speech, not from prior reputation. Its three components are *phronesis*
  (practical good sense), *aretē* (virtue), and *eunoia* (goodwill toward the
  audience). A speaker fails on ethos by seeming foolish, dishonest, or
  indifferent to the hearers' interests — and the three failures need
  different repairs.
- **Pathos** — persuasion by putting the audience into a certain state.
  Book II.2–11 is the earliest systematic psychology of emotion in the Western
  canon: anger, calm, friendship, enmity, fear, confidence, shame,
  benevolence, pity, indignation, envy, emulation. For each Aristotle gives a
  definition, the state of mind of those who feel it, whom they feel it
  toward, and on what grounds. It is a functional analysis — anger is defined
  as desire for revenge for a perceived slight — and is far closer to modern
  appraisal theories of emotion than to anything in between.
- **Logos** — persuasion by the argument itself.

Modern textbooks turn these into a checklist. Aristotle's point is
structural: they are the three places persuasion can come from, given that the
speaker, the hearer, and the speech are the only three elements present.

## Enthymeme and example

The enthymeme is "the body of persuasion" (I.1). It is a rhetorical
syllogism — a deduction from premises the audience already accepts, usually
with one premise unstated because the audience supplies it. It reasons from
what holds *for the most part*, not from necessity, because human affairs do
not admit of necessity.

The unstated premise is the whole mechanism. "She's a doctor, so she'll know"
works only because the hearer supplies "doctors know about illness." The
audience does the completing work, and therefore experiences the conclusion as
partly their own. This is why enthymemes persuade better than fully explicit
syllogisms — and why analysing an argument by *reconstructing its suppressed
premise* is the single most useful critical operation the Rhetoric teaches.

The example (*paradeigma*) is rhetorical induction — argument from a parallel
case, historical or invented (fables, comparisons).

**Topoi** are the "places" or lines of argument one goes to in order to find
enthymemes: argument from opposites, from more and less, from consequences,
from definition, from division, from precedent, from the incentives involved.
Book II.23 lists twenty-eight. They are a heuristic for invention — where to
look for an argument — and they double as a checklist for detecting the moves
in someone else's.

## The three genres

| Genre | Setting | Time | Ends | Audience is |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Deliberative | assembly | future | advantageous / harmful | judge of things to come |
| Forensic | law court | past | just / unjust | judge of things past |
| Epideictic | ceremony | present | honourable / shameful | spectator |

The classification is by what the audience is being asked to do, which is a
better organising principle than most modern taxonomies of communication.

## The catch about style

Book III is on style (*lexis*) and arrangement. Aristotle's attitude is
grudging: "the arts of language cannot help having a small but real
importance... justice should be our sole aim in our speech" — but since
audiences are imperfect, style has to be studied. He recommends clarity and
appropriateness, praises metaphor as the mark of genius and the one thing that
cannot be learnt from another, and dismisses most contemporary theory of
delivery as vulgar but effective.

## The inheritance

Almost every twentieth-century model of persuasion is a re-derivation of
something in the Rhetoric with measurement attached:

- **Source credibility research** (Hovland and the Yale group, 1950s) is
  ethos operationalised — and it recovered Aristotle's split, finding
  expertise and trustworthiness as separable dimensions, matching *phronesis*
  and *aretē*.
- **The Elaboration Likelihood Model** (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986) and the
  **Heuristic–Systematic Model** (Chaiken, 1980) distinguish persuasion via
  argument quality from persuasion via peripheral cues — roughly logos versus
  ethos/pathos, with the addition that which route operates depends on the
  audience's motivation and ability to process.
- **Appraisal theories of emotion** (Lazarus, Scherer, Frijda) reconstruct
  emotions as evaluations of a situation relative to one's goals — which is
  Rhetoric II's method, two and a half thousand years later.
- **Narrative persuasion / transportation** research is the *paradeigma*.

What the modern work adds is measurement and, critically, effect sizes.
What it lost, mostly, is Aristotle's insistence that rhetoric is an analytic
faculty before it is a productive one.

See [persuasion-science] for what the evidence now shows, including where the
popular versions overstate it, and [manipulation-and-defence] for the line
between rhetoric and manipulation — which Aristotle himself does not draw
sharply, and which is arguably the work's main gap.
