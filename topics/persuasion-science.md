# Persuasion: the models and what survived testing

## The dual-process spine

Nearly all modern persuasion theory rests on the observation that attitude
change happens by two different routes, and that which route operates depends
on the audience's motivation and ability to think about the message.

**Elaboration Likelihood Model** (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986). When elaboration
likelihood is high — the topic matters to you, you have the knowledge and the
time — persuasion runs through the *central route*: argument quality
dominates, and the resulting attitude is more persistent, more resistant to
counter-argument, and more predictive of behaviour. When elaboration
likelihood is low, persuasion runs through the *peripheral route*: source
attractiveness, expertise heuristics, message length, mood, number of
arguments rather than their strength. Peripheral attitudes are weaker and
decay faster.

The ELM's most useful and least-quoted claim is that a variable can serve
*different functions at different elaboration levels*. Source expertise acts
as a peripheral cue when you are not thinking; when you are thinking, it acts
as an argument or biases how you process one. So there is no fixed answer to
"does credibility increase persuasion" — it depends on the processing state.

**Heuristic–Systematic Model** (Chaiken, 1980) is the parallel account, with
two differences worth knowing: HSM allows the two modes to operate
*simultaneously* rather than as a trade-off, and it distinguishes accuracy,
defence and impression motives, which the ELM folds together.

Status: the dual-route framework is one of the better-supported structures in
social psychology, though it is a framework rather than a point prediction,
which makes it hard to falsify — a standing criticism (Kruglanski's unimodel
argues the two routes are one process operating on different evidence).

## Cialdini's principles — and their replication record

Robert Cialdini's *Influence* (1984; 2021 edition adds Unity) is the most
widely read work on the subject. The seven principles:

1. **Reciprocity** — a received favour creates felt obligation.
2. **Commitment and consistency** — people act consistently with prior
   public, effortful, freely-chosen commitments.
3. **Social proof** — behaviour is inferred from what similar others do.
4. **Liking** — similarity, compliments, familiarity, cooperation.
5. **Authority** — deference to expertise markers, including symbols.
6. **Scarcity** — value inferred from limited availability; loss framing.
7. **Unity** — shared identity, "we"-ness, not mere similarity.

Where the evidence stands, honestly:

- **Social proof / descriptive norms** is the best-supported. Field
  experiments on energy conservation (Schultz et al., 2007; Allcott's
  large-scale OPOWER studies, n in the millions) show real, replicated
  effects — but small: typically 1–2% reductions in household energy use.
  Real, robust, and much smaller than the popular accounts imply. Note
  Schultz's **boomerang effect**: telling below-average users they are below
  average raised their consumption. Norms cut both ways.
- **Reciprocity** replicates in field settings (the restaurant-mint studies,
  survey-incentive research) with modest effects.
- **Scarcity and loss framing** rest on prospect theory (Kahneman & Tversky,
  1979), which is well-supported in the lab. Loss-aversion's *magnitude*
  (the famous 2:1 ratio) is contested and appears smaller and more
  context-dependent than the canonical figure.
- **Commitment/consistency** is the shakiest. The foot-in-the-door effect
  meta-analyses to a small effect (r ≈ .1); the door-in-the-face effect is
  similarly small and boundary-conditioned. Cialdini's own low-ball procedure
  has had limited independent replication.
- **Authority.** The Milgram obedience studies are the standard citation and
  they need heavy qualification — see below.

The general shape: **the principles are real phenomena, the effect sizes are
small to moderate, and the popular presentation (dramatic anecdote plus
implied large effect) systematically overstates them.** Cialdini's own work is
more careful than its reception.

## The famous studies that need asterisks

**Milgram's obedience experiments (1961–62).** Not a clean demonstration that
65% of people will kill on command. Gina Perry's archival work (*Behind the
Shock Machine*, 2013) found the experimenter deviated from the script and
improvised coercion far beyond the four prescribed prods; that many
participants expressed doubt the shocks were real; and that Milgram reported
selectively from 24 experimental variations with compliance ranging from 0% to
above 90%. Haslam and Reicher's re-analysis argues the results show
*identification with the scientific project* — "engaged followership" —
rather than blind obedience to authority as such. The phenomenon is real;
the standard interpretation is not established.

**Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment (1971).** Now substantially
discredited as evidence for situational role-adoption. Le Texier's archival
work (2019) and released tapes show guards were coached toward brutality,
that a key "breakdown" was later described by the participant as faked to
leave, and that there was no control condition, no hypothesis test, and a
sample of nine guards. It should be taught as a case study in research ethics
and in how a compelling narrative outruns its evidence — not as a finding.

**Ego depletion.** The claim that self-control draws on a limited resource had
a large early literature and a widely-cited meta-analysis. A pre-registered
multi-lab replication (Hagger et al., 2016; 23 labs) found an effect
indistinguishable from zero. Do not build on it.

**Social priming (elderly-words-slow-walking and similar).** Largely failed to
replicate. The broader "behavioural priming" literature is one of the main
casualties of the replication crisis.

## What holds up better than the famous stuff

- **Inoculation theory** (McGuire, 1961) — pre-exposure to a weakened
  counter-argument plus refutation builds resistance to later persuasion.
  This has had a genuine revival in misinformation research: van der Linden,
  Roozenbeek and colleagues have run large field tests (including a YouTube
  study with ~5 million users) showing prebunking improves manipulation-
  technique recognition, with small but real and replicated effects. It is
  the best-evidenced *defensive* intervention available.
- **Narrative transportation** (Green & Brock) — absorption in a story
  reduces counter-arguing. Moderate, replicated.
- **Descriptive vs injunctive norms** distinction (Cialdini, Reno & Kallgren)
  — well supported.
- **Deep canvassing** (Broockman & Kalla, *Science*, 2016) — non-judgemental,
  perspective-taking conversation produced durable reductions in prejudice
  lasting months, in a pre-registered field experiment. Notable both for the
  result and for the context: it was the replacement study after the
  fraudulent LaCour paper it was checking. It is also, interestingly, a
  persuasion method built on *not* using the compliance techniques.

## The self-persuasion finding

The most reliably large effect in the field is not a technique applied to
someone — it is getting someone to argue a position themselves. Cognitive
dissonance research (Festinger & Carlsmith, 1959, and the large induced-
compliance literature that followed) shows that people who generate their own
arguments change more, and more durably, than people who receive good ones.
Motivational interviewing (Miller & Rollnick), which has solid clinical trial
support for behaviour change, is built entirely on this: elicit change talk
from the person rather than supplying it.

This is worth sitting with. The single most effective thing in the persuasion
literature is asking questions and listening — which is also the thing that
does not work if the person's autonomy is not genuinely respected, because
the mechanism *is* their own reasoning.
