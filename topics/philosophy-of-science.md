# Philosophy of science

This is the theory behind the practice in [research-methods]. A student who
knows only the methods checklist will apply it mechanically; a student who
knows why the checklist exists can tell when it is being gamed.

## The problem of induction

**Hume**: no number of observed instances licenses a universal conclusion, and
any attempt to justify induction by its past success is circular. The problem
has never been solved, only relocated.

Responses worth knowing: Popper's (abandon induction entirely — science is
deductive falsification), the pragmatic vindication (Reichenbach — if any
method works, induction works), Goodman's **new riddle** (why project "green"
rather than "grue"? — showing that the problem is not confirmation but which
predicates are projectible), and the Bayesian reframing (induction is
probability updating, and the question becomes where priors come from).

## Demarcation

**Popper** (*Logik der Forschung*, 1934): what separates science from
pseudo-science is **falsifiability**. A theory is scientific if it forbids
something — if there is an observation that would refute it. His targets were
Marxism and psychoanalysis, which he judged unfalsifiable because they could
accommodate any outcome. Science proceeds by bold conjecture and severe
attempted refutation; corroboration is never confirmation.

The criterion is too crude as stated, and its problems matter:

- **The Duhem–Quine thesis**: hypotheses are never tested in isolation. A
  failed prediction impugns the conjunction of hypothesis, auxiliary
  assumptions, instrument theory and background conditions — and logic alone
  cannot say which to give up. Any theory can be saved by adjusting something
  else. So a clean falsification is not available.
- Probabilistic and statistical claims are not strictly falsifiable at all,
  which excludes most of the sciences of interest here.
- Historically, scientists routinely and rightly retain refuted theories.

**Lakatos** repaired it with **research programmes**: a hard core of
commitments protected by a belt of auxiliary hypotheses. What matters is
whether the programme is *progressive* (predicting novel facts that are then
confirmed) or *degenerating* (making only post hoc adjustments). This is much
closer to how one should judge a body of work, and it is the right lens for
the psychology literature in [research-methods] — a programme that only ever
explains its failures after the fact is degenerating.

**Kuhn** (*The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*, 1962) rejected the whole
framing. Science mostly proceeds as **normal science**: puzzle-solving within
a **paradigm** that supplies problems, methods and standards. Anomalies
accumulate; crisis; **revolution**; a new paradigm. Because paradigms carry
their own standards, competing ones are **incommensurable** — there is no
neutral algorithm for choosing. Kuhn's later work softened this into shared
values (accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness) that are
weighted differently by different scientists.

**Feyerabend** (*Against Method*) pushed to "anything goes" — no methodological
rule has gone unviolated in a productive episode. Best read as a corrective to
tidy reconstructions rather than a position.

## Realism and its rivals

**Scientific realism**: successful theories are approximately true and their
unobservable entities exist. The main argument is the **no-miracles argument**
— realism is the only view that does not make the success of science a
coincidence.

Against it:

- **Pessimistic meta-induction** (Laudan): the history of science is a
  graveyard of successful-then-abandoned theories (phlogiston, caloric, the
  ether). Induction on that record suggests current theories are false too.
- **Underdetermination**: empirically equivalent rivals are always available
  in principle.
- **Constructive empiricism** (van Fraassen): the aim is empirical adequacy,
  not truth; acceptance of a theory involves no belief about unobservables.
- **Structural realism** (Worrall): what is retained across theory change is
  mathematical structure, not ontology — a middle path.

## Explanation

- **Deductive-nomological** (Hempel): to explain is to derive the event from
  laws plus initial conditions. Famous counterexamples — the flagpole and its
  shadow (the derivation runs both ways, but only one direction explains), the
  barometer and the storm (correlation without explanation).
- **Causal-mechanical** (Salmon, Woodward): explanation traces causal
  processes. Woodward's **interventionist** account — X explains Y if
  intervening on X changes Y — is now the most influential, and it connects
  directly to the logic of experimentation.
- **Unificationist** (Kitcher): explanation is reduction of the number of
  independent phenomena we must accept.
- **Statistical relevance**: explanation makes the outcome more probable, or
  at least changes its probability. Necessary for the sciences here, where
  nothing is deterministic.

## Statistical inference, philosophically

Directly relevant to [research-methods]:

- **Frequentist / error-statistical** (Mayo): a claim is warranted to the
  extent it has passed a **severe test** — one it would very probably have
  failed had it been false. This is the best philosophical articulation of
  why p-hacking is a genuine epistemic wrong rather than a rule violation:
  a test bent to pass is not severe, so passing it says nothing.
- **Bayesian**: belief as probability, updated on evidence. Elegant, and
  contested on where priors come from and what "the probability of a
  hypothesis" means.
- **Likelihoodism**: evidence favours whichever hypothesis makes it more
  probable; no priors, no error rates.

Mayo's severity criterion is the single most useful import from this field
into everyday reading of studies. Ask not "was it significant" but "would this
procedure have caught the claim if it were false?"

## Values in science

The **value-free ideal** — that science should be free of moral and political
values — is largely abandoned in its strong form. **Inductive risk** (Rudner,
Douglas): deciding how much evidence is enough before accepting a hypothesis
requires weighing the costs of being wrong in each direction, which is a value
judgement internal to the method. **Longino** argues objectivity is a property
of communities with effective mechanisms of criticism, not of individuals.

This is not relativism, and it should not be read as one: it says that the
place to look for objectivity is the social structure of criticism — peer
review, replication, adversarial collaboration, preregistration — which is
exactly what the reforms in [research-methods] rebuild.
