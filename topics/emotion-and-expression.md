# Emotion

## The oldest account is still in play

Aristotle's *Rhetoric* II defines each emotion by its **appraisal**: anger is
distress at a perceived unjustified slight, coupled with desire for revenge;
fear is distress at an imagined imminent destructive evil; pity is distress at
undeserved misfortune befalling someone who could be oneself. He then gives,
for each, the state of mind of those who feel it and the people and grounds
toward which they feel it.

That structure — emotion as an evaluation of a situation relative to one's
concerns — is essentially modern appraisal theory. It is worth noticing that
the field's dominant contemporary framework was available in 340 BCE and that
the intervening tradition largely lost it.

## The four families of theory

**Basic emotion theory** (Tomkins, Izard, Ekman). A small set of discrete,
evolved, pan-cultural emotions, each with a characteristic neural signature,
physiology, and facial expression. Ekman's canonical six: anger, disgust,
fear, happiness, sadness, surprise. The cross-cultural recognition studies
(including with the Fore of Papua New Guinea) were the empirical foundation.

**Constructionism** (Lisa Feldman Barrett, *How Emotions Are Made*, 2017;
also Russell's core affect model). There are no emotion "fingerprints".
The brain constructs emotional episodes from core affect (valence and
arousal) plus conceptual knowledge supplied by culture and language. The
categories are learned, not given. Evidence: meta-analyses find no consistent,
specific autonomic or neural signature per emotion category; facial
configurations vary widely within a category and recur across categories; the
original cross-cultural studies used forced-choice designs that inflate
apparent recognition, and free-labelling designs recover much lower agreement.

**Appraisal theory** (Arnold, Lazarus, Scherer, Frijda, Roseman). Emotions are
differentiated by dimensions of evaluation: novelty, pleasantness, goal
relevance, agency and blame, coping potential, norm compatibility. Scherer's
Component Process Model is the most developed. This family is the most
productive for research because it makes graded, testable predictions and
explains why the same event produces different emotions in different people.

**Psychological construction of a different kind** — the James–Lange lineage
(feeling is the perception of bodily change), revived in Damasio's somatic
marker hypothesis and in interoception research. Barrett's account absorbs
much of this.

## Where the dispute stands

The strong basic-emotion claim is in serious trouble. Barrett, Adolphs,
Marsella, Martinez & Pollak (*Psychological Science in the Public Interest*,
2019) — a deliberately adversarial author team including basic-emotion
sympathisers — reviewed the evidence and concluded that the common view, that
emotional state can be read from facial movement, is not supported at the
level of confidence its commercial and legal applications assume.

What is agreed by nearly everyone: facial movements are informative; some
regularities are widely shared; emotion has real biological substrate; context
massively determines interpretation. What is disputed: whether the categories
are natural kinds with signatures, or learned concepts imposed on continuous
affect.

The practical upshot is the same under either theory: **you cannot reliably
infer a specific emotion from a facial configuration without context.** This
matters because emotion-recognition AI is being sold into hiring, border
control, education and policing on precisely that assumption. See
[nonverbal-behaviour].

## Regulation

**Gross's process model** is the standard framework and the most useful
practical content in the area. Five families of strategy, ordered by where
they intervene in the emotion-generative sequence:

1. *Situation selection* — approach or avoid.
2. *Situation modification* — change the circumstances.
3. *Attentional deployment* — distraction, rumination (the maladaptive form).
4. *Cognitive change* — reappraisal: reinterpreting meaning.
5. *Response modulation* — suppression: altering the expression or
   physiology after the emotion is underway.

The consistent finding: **reappraisal is generally adaptive** (lower
physiological cost, better affect, better social outcomes) while
**expressive suppression is generally costly** (unchanged or increased
physiological arousal, worse memory for the interaction, worse relationship
outcomes). The caveat added by later work: this depends on context — in
uncontrollable situations, and in some cultural contexts where suppression is
normative, the difference narrows or reverses.

**Affect labelling** — putting feelings into words — reliably reduces
amygdala response and self-reported distress (Lieberman et al.). One of the
better-supported small interventions.

**Alexithymia** — difficulty identifying and describing one's own emotions —
is measurable (TAS-20) and predicts poorer regulation. Barrett's related
construct, *emotional granularity* (the fineness of one's emotion vocabulary
and categorisation), predicts better regulation outcomes. If constructionism
is right, this is causally important rather than incidental: having more
precise emotion concepts changes what you feel, not just what you can say.

## Measurement

- **Self-report**: PANAS, DES, the affect grid; ecological momentary
  assessment for real-time sampling.
- **Physiological**: skin conductance, heart-rate variability, facial EMG
  (corrugator and zygomaticus are the workhorses), startle modulation.
  None is emotion-specific.
- **Behavioural**: FACS coding, vocal acoustics, body movement.
- The **honest constraint**: no measure identifies a discrete emotion.
  Converging measures constrain valence and arousal well; category
  identification remains inference.

## Emotion in the persuasion literature

Aristotle's pathos, operationalised: fear appeals work when the threat is
credible *and* an efficacious response is offered (Tannenbaum et al., 2015,
meta-analysis) — otherwise they produce defensive avoidance. Anger increases
heuristic processing and risk-seeking; sadness increases systematic processing
and risk-aversion (Lerner & Keltner's appraisal-tendency framework). Incidental
emotion — emotion from an unrelated source — carries over into unrelated
judgements, which is the mechanism behind a great deal of both advertising and
courtroom strategy.
