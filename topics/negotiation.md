# Negotiation

Negotiation is the one applied area in this corpus where the research and the
practical advice largely agree, because the outcomes are measurable and the
experiments are cheap to run.

## The two structures

**Distributive** (claiming value) — a fixed pie; one side's gain is the
other's loss. Governed by the **ZOPA** (zone of possible agreement) between
the two parties' reservation values.

**Integrative** (creating value) — differences in priorities, risk tolerance,
time preference or forecasts allow trades that make both sides better off.
Most real negotiations are mixed-motive: integrative in structure, distributive
in the endgame.

The central empirical finding of the field is the **fixed-pie bias**:
negotiators systematically assume interests are directly opposed when they are
not, and therefore fail to find available joint gains. In studies where
compatible issues exist — both parties want the same outcome — negotiators
routinely fail to identify them, sometimes trading them away. This is the
single most valuable thing to know.

## The core apparatus

**BATNA** — Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement (Fisher & Ury, *Getting
to Yes*, 1981). Not a bargaining position but a fact about the world: what
happens if no deal is reached. Two consequences that follow and that most
people miss:

1. Your power in a negotiation is a function of your BATNA, not of your
   assertiveness. Improving the BATNA is done *away from the table*, before
   the negotiation, and is almost always higher-leverage than any tactic used
   at it.
2. You should estimate the other side's BATNA. Most preparation failure is
   failure to model their alternatives.

**Reservation value** — the walk-away point, derived from the BATNA.
**Aspiration** — the target. Research consistently finds that specific,
justifiable, ambitious aspirations produce better outcomes than "do your
best," matching Locke & Latham's goal-setting findings elsewhere.

**Principled negotiation** (the *Getting to Yes* framework):
- Separate the people from the problem.
- Focus on interests, not positions. (The orange parable: two people fight
  over one orange, split it, and discover one wanted the juice and the other
  the zest. Positions were opposed; interests were compatible.)
- Invent options for mutual gain before deciding.
- Insist on objective criteria — market rates, precedent, expert standards —
  so that concession is to a principle rather than to pressure.

The framework's known weakness, raised repeatedly since 1981, is that it
assumes a good-faith counterpart. Ury's *Getting Past No* and Mnookin's
*Bargaining with the Devil* address the hard cases; the honest answer to
"what if they refuse to negotiate in good faith" is usually "improve your
BATNA and be willing to use it."

## What the experiments show

- **Anchoring works, and it is large.** The first offer strongly predicts the
  settlement (Galinsky & Mussweiler and a substantial literature). This is one
  of the effects that survived the replication crisis. The corollary matters
  as much: the defence against an anchor is to have generated your own target
  and your BATNA *before* hearing theirs, and to re-anchor explicitly rather
  than counter incrementally.
- **First offers.** Making the first offer generally helps *when you are
  well informed* about the ZOPA, and hurts when you are not — because an
  uninformed first offer either leaves money on the table or reveals your
  ignorance.
- **Information exchange creates value.** Sharing priorities (not reservation
  values) reliably increases joint outcomes. Negotiators are systematically too
  reluctant to do it.
- **MESOs** — Multiple Equivalent Simultaneous Offers. Presenting two or three
  packages of equal value to you reveals the other side's priorities without
  conceding, and is one of the better-evidenced practical techniques.
- **Contingent contracts** resolve disagreements about facts by betting on
  them rather than arguing about them.
- **Questions beat arguments.** Asking about interests outperforms asserting
  positions, in line with the self-persuasion findings in
  [persuasion-science].
- **Accountability and audiences** make negotiators more competitive and less
  likely to find integrative solutions.
- **Reactive devaluation** — the same proposal is valued lower when it comes
  from an adversary. A real obstacle, and a reason to have terms generated
  jointly or by a third party.

## The ethics, treated as part of the subject

Negotiation research takes deception seriously as a research topic, and the
distinctions are worth having:

- **Misrepresenting your reservation value** is near-universal, widely
  expected, and legally treated as "puffery" in most jurisdictions.
- **Misrepresenting material facts** is fraud.
- The professional norms (Lewicki's work on ethically ambiguous tactics)
  distinguish traditional competitive bargaining from emotional manipulation,
  misrepresentation, inappropriate information gathering, and bluffing about
  one's constituency.

Where negotiation tactics cross into the territory of
[manipulation-and-defence]: manufactured deadlines, fabricated competing
offers, engineered exhaustion, good-cop/bad-cop framing designed to induce
relief-based concession, and the "nibble" after agreement. Each fails the
disclosure test — they stop working if named — which is exactly the diagnostic
to apply, and the reason a negotiator who recognises them can usually neutralise
one by naming it aloud.

## Reading

- Fisher, Ury & Patton, *Getting to Yes* (3rd ed.) — the framework.
- Malhotra & Bazerman, *Negotiation Genius* (2007) — the best synthesis of
  the experimental literature into practice.
- Thompson, *The Mind and Heart of the Negotiator* — the standard textbook,
  research-dense.
- Raiffa, *The Art and Science of Negotiation* — the formal/decision-analytic
  treatment.
- Voss, *Never Split the Difference* — widely read; the tactical advice is
  drawn from hostage negotiation and is largely untested in controlled
  research. Read it after the others, and treat its claims as hypotheses.
