# Political theory

## Aristotle's Politics

The *Politics* is the sequel to the *Ethics* — explicitly. The Ethics ends by
saying that since legislation shapes character, the inquiry must continue into
constitutions. This is the structural claim: **politics is the completion of
ethics, because the polis is where character is formed.**

Key positions:

- *Man is by nature a political animal* (*zōon politikon*, I.2). Not a claim
  about sociability but about completion: the polis exists for the sake of
  living well, and a being who could live outside it would be "either a beast
  or a god." The individual is not prior to the community.
- *The polis is prior to the individual* in the order of nature, as the whole
  is prior to the part — a hand severed from a body is a hand in name only.
- **The classification of constitutions** (III.7), by who rules and whether
  they rule in the common interest:

| | Correct (common interest) | Deviant (rulers' interest) |
|---|---|---|
| One | kingship | tyranny |
| Few | aristocracy | oligarchy |
| Many | polity (*politeia*) | democracy |

  Note that Aristotle's "democracy" is a *deviant* form — rule by the poor in
  their own interest. His preferred practicable regime is *polity*, a mixture
  of oligarchic and democratic elements resting on a large middle class,
  because "the best political community is formed by citizens of the middle
  class." This is the ancestor of mixed-constitution and separation-of-powers
  thinking.
- **The wisdom of the multitude** (III.11) — the many, collectively, may
  judge better than the few experts, as a potluck feast may be better than one
  provided at one man's expense. An early and genuinely interesting argument
  for democratic epistemics, revived in contemporary work by Estlund, Landemore
  and in the Condorcet jury theorem literature.
- **The defence of natural slavery** (I.4–7). It must be read and named, not
  skipped: Aristotle argues some humans are slaves by nature, lacking the
  deliberative faculty in authoritative form. The argument is bad on its own
  terms — he concedes many actual slaves are not natural slaves — and it is
  the clearest case in the canon of a great philosopher rationalising his
  society's institution. Teaching it honestly is part of teaching him.
- **Revolution and stability** (Book V) — a systematic empirical study of why
  constitutions fall, based on his collection of 158 constitutions. Largely
  about perceived injustice in the distribution of honour and office.

## The main line since

**Machiavelli** (*The Prince*, 1513; *Discourses*, 1517). Usually read as the
severing of politics from ethics — the ruler must learn "how not to be good."
The *Discourses* complicate this: there Machiavelli is a republican who
prizes liberty, civic virtue (*virtù*) and the productive conflict between
patricians and plebs. The two works together pose the enduring problem of
dirty hands: whether political action has its own morality.

**Hobbes** (*Leviathan*, 1651). From the state of nature — "solitary, poor,
nasty, brutish, and short" — to the covenant establishing an absolute
sovereign. The first fully individualist derivation of political authority: the
state is justified by what rational self-interested agents would agree to.
Everything afterwards is a response.

**Locke** (*Two Treatises*, 1689). Natural rights to life, liberty and
property prior to government; government as trustee; the right of revolution
when trust is broken. The labour theory of property acquisition and its
proviso ("enough and as good left for others") is where most of the critical
attention now sits.

**Rousseau** (*The Social Contract*, 1762). The general will; the claim that
legitimate law is what a people gives itself; "forced to be free" as the most
contested sentence in political philosophy. Also the sharpest pre-Marxian
critique of inequality (*Second Discourse*).

**Mill** (*On Liberty*, 1859). The harm principle: the only legitimate ground
for coercing an individual is preventing harm to others. Also the argument
from truth for free expression — that suppressing an opinion robs us either of
a truth or of the clearer perception of truth that collision with error
produces. Directly relevant to [manipulation-and-defence]: Mill's defence of
liberty presumes people reason, which is why manipulation rather than
censorship is arguably the harder problem for his framework.

**Marx.** Historical materialism; ideology as the ideas of the ruling class;
alienation; the critique of the liberal separation of political and economic
freedom. Whatever one's view of the programme, the analytical claim — that
political ideas serve material interests and that this is often invisible to
those who hold them — permanently changed the field.

**Rawls** (*A Theory of Justice*, 1971). Justice as fairness, derived from
the original position behind a veil of ignorance. Two principles, lexically
ordered: equal basic liberties, then fair equality of opportunity plus the
difference principle (inequalities permitted only if they benefit the least
advantaged). *Political Liberalism* (1993) reframes this for a society of
reasonable but incompatible comprehensive doctrines — the overlapping
consensus. The single most influential work of twentieth-century political
philosophy, and the thing everyone since argues with.

**The main replies to Rawls:**
- Nozick (*Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, 1974) — entitlement theory; the Wilt
  Chamberlain argument that patterned principles of distribution require
  continuous interference with liberty.
- Sen and Nussbaum — the capabilities approach: measure justice by what
  people are actually able to do and be, not by primary goods.
- Communitarians (Sandel, MacIntyre, Taylor, Walzer) — the unencumbered self
  of the original position is a fiction; identity is constituted by ends we do
  not choose. This is, in effect, Aristotle's objection.
- Feminist political theory (Okin, Pateman, Young) — the public/private
  distinction shelters the family from justice; the "sexual contract"
  underlying the social contract.
- Critical race theory (Mills, *The Racial Contract*, 1997) — the social
  contract tradition as historically an agreement among some persons about the
  status of others.

**Arendt** (*The Origins of Totalitarianism*, 1951; *The Human Condition*,
1958; *Eichmann in Jerusalem*, 1963). Totalitarianism as a novel form
resting on loneliness and ideology rather than ordinary tyranny; the
distinction between labour, work and action; the "banality of evil" — a
phrase widely misunderstood as excusing perpetrators, where her claim was
about the absence of thought rather than the absence of guilt. Her account of
Eichmann specifically has been challenged by later archival work (Stangneth's
*Eichmann Before Jerusalem*), while the conceptual point stands independently.

**Foucault.** Power as productive and capillary rather than merely
repressive; discipline and the panopticon; governmentality; the co-production
of power and knowledge. The most cited framework for analysing institutions,
and the most frequently applied loosely.

**Habermas.** The public sphere and its structural transformation;
communicative action and the ideal speech situation as a standard against
which actual discourse can be measured — which makes his framework unusually
useful for diagnosing manipulation, since manipulation is precisely
communication that could not survive the conditions of the ideal speech
situation.

## Contemporary empirical questions worth study

- **Affective polarization** — growing dislike of the out-party more than
  disagreement on policy (Iyengar, Sood & Lelkes). Better documented in the US
  than elsewhere; cross-national evidence is mixed.
- **Democratic backsliding** — Levitsky & Ziblatt on the erosion of unwritten
  norms; V-Dem's measurement programme.
- **Misinformation and its actual effects** — the honest state of the field is
  that exposure is concentrated in small, already-partisan audiences and
  persuasive effects are smaller than the discourse assumes (Guess et al.).
  Treat alarmist and dismissive accounts with equal suspicion.
- **Deliberative mini-publics and citizens' assemblies** — the strongest
  empirical support for deliberative democratic theory; participants do change
  views under structured deliberation (Fishkin's Deliberative Polling).
