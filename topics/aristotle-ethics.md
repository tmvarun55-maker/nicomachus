# The Nicomachean Ethics

The work is named for Nicomachus — either Aristotle's father or, more likely on
the usual reading, his son, who may have edited it. It is lecture material, not
a polished treatise, which is why it circles back on itself and why some books
are shared with the Eudemian Ethics.

## The function argument

Aristotle opens with the observation that every craft and inquiry aims at some
good, and asks whether there is a good pursued for its own sake and never for
the sake of anything further. He answers: eudaimonia. The word is badly served
by "happiness" — it is not a feeling but a way a whole life goes. "Flourishing"
is closer; "living well and doing well" is Aristotle's own gloss.

The argument for what eudaimonia consists in is the *ergon* or function
argument (I.7). A flautist's good lies in flute-playing done well; an eye's
good in seeing well. What is the characteristic activity of a human being?
Not nutrition and growth — plants do that. Not perception — animals do that.
What remains is activity of the part of the soul that has reason. So the human
good is *activity of soul in accordance with virtue*, and if there are several
virtues, in accordance with the best and most complete, over a complete life.

The last clause matters: "one swallow does not make a spring." Eudaimonia is
not a moment or a mood. It is a shape a life has.

## Virtue as a mean

A virtue of character (*ēthikē aretē*) is a stable disposition (*hexis*) to
feel and act in the right way. Aristotle's famous doctrine (II.6) is that each
such virtue lies in a mean between an excess and a deficiency:

| Sphere | Deficiency | Virtue | Excess |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fear and confidence | cowardice | courage | rashness |
| Pleasure and pain | insensibility | temperance | self-indulgence |
| Giving and taking money | meanness | generosity | prodigality |
| Honour, small scale | pusillanimity | proper ambition | over-ambition |
| Anger | inirascibility | good temper | irascibility |
| Truth about oneself | self-deprecation | truthfulness | boastfulness |
| Social conduct | quarrelsomeness | friendliness | obsequiousness |

Four things are routinely misread here.

First, the mean is **relative to us**, not an arithmetic midpoint. The right
amount of food for a wrestler is not the right amount for a beginner.

Second, the mean is not moderation for its own sake. Aristotle is explicit
(II.6, 1107a) that some actions and passions admit of no mean at all —
spite, shamelessness, envy; adultery, theft, murder. There is no "right amount"
of these. The doctrine is about the spheres where excess and deficiency are
both real failures, not a general recommendation of the middling.

Third, virtue concerns feeling as much as action: to feel anger "at the right
things and towards the right people, in the right way, at the right time, and
for the right length of time." A person who does the right thing while
resenting it has not yet got the virtue.

Fourth, virtue is acquired by habituation, not instruction. "We become just by
doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts"
(II.1). This creates the well-known circularity — how can you do just acts
before you are just? — which Aristotle resolves by distinguishing acts that are
*of a certain kind* from acts done *as a just person would do them*: knowingly,
chosen for their own sake, and from a firm disposition.

## Phronesis — practical wisdom

The intellectual virtue that governs the whole system is *phronesis*, practical
wisdom (Book VI). It is not cleverness (*deinotēs*), which is mere skill at
hitting whatever target one has; the clever person can be a scoundrel.
Phronesis is the capacity to deliberate well about what conduces to living
well overall — and it is inseparable from good character, because a corrupt
person's view of the end is itself corrupted.

This is the deep structural claim of the Ethics: **you cannot have practical
wisdom without moral virtue, and you cannot have full moral virtue without
practical wisdom.** They are two aspects of one achievement. It is also why
Aristotle refuses to give rules. The mean is determined "by reason, and as the
person of practical wisdom would determine it" — a standard that names a
person rather than a formula, on purpose.

## Akrasia — weakness of will

Book VII takes up Socrates' claim that no one does wrong knowingly. Aristotle
finds this "plainly at variance with the observed facts" but wants to preserve
what is right in it. His solution turns on kinds of knowing. The akratic
person has the universal premise ("sweet things should not be eaten now") and
even the particular ("this is sweet"), but under the influence of appetite the
knowledge is present only in the way a sleeping or drunk person's knowledge is
present — possessed but not exercised. So Socrates is right that in the moment
of action, full knowledge is not operative.

Compare: the *akratic* knows better and fails; the *akolastos*
(self-indulgent) has the wrong values and does not fail by their own lights;
the *enkratic* (continent) has bad desires but masters them; the temperate
person no longer has the bad desires. Only the last is fully virtuous.

## Friendship and the human good

Books VIII and IX — nearly a fifth of the work — are on *philia*, a word
covering friendship, affection, and the bonds of family, city and business.
Three kinds: friendships of utility, of pleasure, and of character. Only the
last is friendship "without qualification," because only there is the friend
loved for what they are rather than for what they provide. Such friendships
are rare, slow to form, and require living together — "you cannot know each
other until you have eaten the proverbial salt together."

Aristotle argues the good person needs friends even though they are
self-sufficient, and the argument is not sentimental: we perceive our own
existence best through shared activity with someone whose character we can
read, and a friend is "another self."

## The contemplative life problem

Book X ends by ranking *theōria*, contemplation, above the practical virtues —
the most self-sufficient, continuous, and pleasant activity, the one closest
to the divine. This sits awkwardly with the rest of the work, which grounds
the human good in the composite of reason and feeling and in a life among
others. Scholars split into "inclusivists" (eudaimonia includes the moral
virtues, with contemplation the highest component) and "intellectualists"
(contemplation just is the human good, with the moral virtues instrumental).
The text supports both readings, which is itself the interesting fact.

## Why it still matters

Modern virtue ethics dates from G.E.M. Anscombe's 1958 "Modern Moral
Philosophy," which argued that concepts like "moral obligation" are survivals
of a legal conception of ethics that has lost its lawgiver, and that we should
return to the psychology of character. Alasdair MacIntyre's *After Virtue*
(1981), Philippa Foot's *Natural Goodness* (2001), and Rosalind Hursthouse's
*On Virtue Ethics* (1999) built the position out.

The standing objection is the **situationist challenge**: work by Doris and
Harman drawing on social psychology argued that behaviour is driven far more
by situation than by stable traits, so the character traits virtue ethics
presupposes may not exist. This debate has since been complicated on both
sides — several of the studies invoked (notably Milgram's and Zimbardo's) are
now themselves contested, and personality psychology's evidence for stable
traits with real predictive validity is stronger than the situationists
allowed. See [research-methods] and [psychology-foundations].

## Reading order

1. Book I (the good, eudaimonia, the function argument)
2. Book II (virtue, habituation, the mean)
3. Book VI (intellectual virtues, phronesis)
4. Book III.1–5 (voluntary action, choice, responsibility)
5. Books VIII–IX (friendship)
6. Book X (pleasure, contemplation)

Irwin's Hackett translation has the best notes for a first reading; Broadie
and Rowe's is the best for the philosophy; Ross (revised Urmson) is the
default in the Revised Oxford Translation and is what most public-domain
editions reproduce.
